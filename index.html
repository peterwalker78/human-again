The problem

Most screens are built to keep you.

You open the laptop to check one thing. You look up and an hour has gone, and whatever you sat down to do is still waiting.

That isn't a lack of willpower. Feeds, notifications and autoplay are designed, tested and tuned by very large teams to hold on to you, because your attention is what they sell. Every other piece of software on your computer treats that hour exactly like an hour spent learning, making something, or talking to someone you love.

47 seconds

How long, on average, people stayed on one screen before switching, in workplace studies since 2016. In 2003 it was about two and a half minutes.

Gloria Mark, Attention Span, 2023
More stress

Interrupted people make up the time by working faster, and pay for it in stress, frustration and time pressure.

Mark, Gudith & Klocke, CHI 2008
In breach

In February 2026 the European Commission's preliminary view was that TikTok's infinite scroll, autoplay and push notifications breach EU law.

European Commission, 6 Feb 2026
What we believe
Software should be judged by how well you live when you're not using it.

So we're building the other kind: three free apps that guard your attention while you work, tell you the truth about your time online, and, when the day is done, send you outside.

The desktop

The desktop that's on your side.

A keyboard-first desktop for Linux, in one download that installs beside the one you have.

It holds your notifications until you pause, shows you what's going on without asking for a look, and measures nothing about you. Good-looking enough that you want to sit down at it; quiet enough that you get lost in the work rather than the machine.

Concentration first

Nothing interrupts you.

You know the moment: halfway through a thought, something pops up, and the thought is gone. Slipstream can tell that you're typing, never what, and keeps that moment yours.

  • Notifications wait for a pause, then arrive together as one card. Urgent ones never wait, and nothing waits more than fifteen minutes.
  • Windows you didn't ask for can't barge in or run off with your keyboard. They pour into a stream at the edge of the screen instead.
  • Hold Caps Lock for Awake, and the screen won't fade or lock by itself until you hold it again.
  • Nothing records your screen without asking you first, by name.

What you put away keeps you posted.

Put a window away and it pours down the side of the screen as a stream of light, still running.

A busy app pours fast and bright; a quiet one sends a few slow grey streaks. A download finishing catches your eye without you going to look.

Rest the pointer on a stream to see what it's using right now. Click it to bring the window back.

No telemetry, ever. What you do on your computer stays on your computer. It never checks for updates by itself.

No AI features, ever, and nothing built to keep you looking for longer than you meant to.

The keyboard

Every window, one key away.

One shortcut tips the whole desktop back. Press a letter and you land on that window.

  • Keys your hands already know. Windows' keys from the start, or a Mac's or Omarchy's habits instead, and every hint on screen follows your choice.
  • Tiling that isn't all or nothing. Hold Super+T and pick an arrangement from pictures of your own windows.
  • An explorer that answers. Tap Super and type an app, a setting, a file, 15% of 80 or 5 km in miles.
  • New to tiling? Take the tour. Fourteen short lessons start from the keys you know, say why before what to press, and play out on a miniature of your own desktop.
The living wallpaper

Step away, and it comes alive.

There are twenty wallpapers, and most aren't animations at all: they're worked out live as you watch, so they never run quite the same way twice. Whichever you choose runs behind your windows all along, and takes the screen when you step away. Every effect has a reduced-motion version, switched live from Settings.

Make it yours

Plugins beside the clock.

Little apps, your own or other people's, each add a card to a panel that drops from the bar. Anything that prints a line of JSON can be one.

Nothing runs until you've looked. Each plugin runs in a sandbox that reaches only what it lists, and Settings says in plain words what that is before you turn it on. If its files change later, it switches itself off until you've looked again.

Your colours, everywhere. Pick one of ten sets of three colours that go together, and the whole desktop follows, Settings included.

Get Slipstream

One command downloads the newest release, checks its published checksum and signature, and says what it will do before it writes anything. Then log out and choose Slipstream on the login screen. Add -- --try to open it in a window first, installing nothing.

The web browser

Glimmerwood

The web browser that's on your side. Probably the only browser that's happiest when you're not using it.

A small, glowing flame with a face lives in the corner of the toolbar. It's yours, and it reflects the quality of your time online the way the sky reflects the weather.

Somewhere that restores you and it brightens and lets off little motes of light. Sink into an endless feed and it clouds over, grows tired and drifts to the edge of its nook, as if it's ready to go outside and hoping you'll come too. It never scolds you.

A garden that grows on good days.

Every new tab opens on a calm page made on your computer. A quiet day brings moss, a day of learning ferns, a calm night fireflies. Nothing ever wilts, and missing a day costs nothing. Instead of the sites you visit most, it suggests somewhere worth going: over 300 researched corners of the web, from live nature cams and free books to museums and gentle puzzles.

See your days, not a scoreboard.

Click the wisp for how today has felt, the shape of your week, and which places restored you or wore you down. No minutes, scores or percentages anywhere: just the pattern, and the kind of mirror that makes the next choice easier. Oh, Thursday evenings are always like that.

Why a little light works better than a lock.

  • Noticing, not nagging. The wisp does the noticing, so the choice stays yours.
  • You're always in charge. No page is ever blocked and nothing pops up to warn you.
  • Time away counts most. The best thing for your wisp isn't a “good” website; it's closing the laptop.
  • Your evenings matter. After eleven, the wisp winds down with you.

Nothing leaves your computer.

  • No accounts, telemetry, update pings or cloud. The only traffic is the pages you visit.
  • The wisp never sees an address, only which list a place was on.
  • History keeps a week, on your computer, then it's deleted.
  • Trackers are blocked before they can load.

Get Glimmerwood

Install the Flatpak, make it your default browser, and use the web as you normally would. There's nothing to set up.

The end of the day

Westering

Going west, as the Sun and stars do when they go down.

Tonight's real sky, and then the real sky outside.

A small stargazing game that is designed to let you go. A few quiet minutes at the end of the day to come back to what matters: the people who are there for you, something to look forward to, and setting down what's weighing on you so it's lighter in the morning.

It draws the sky over you right now, in fine dots: the real stars, the Moon at its real phase, the planets that are really up. The wisp from Glimmerwood is out for the night, on a tuft of moss on the horizon.

Every evening

Four parts, then goodnight.

i

Set it down

Anything heavy tonight? Write it in a few words and it becomes a warm star hung low in the west. Only you will ever see it.

ii

Explore

Tonight's list with the wisp: planets, clusters and galaxies to find, a story told a card at a time, and now and then a small question about your life.

iii

Look up

The sky on your own. Zoom in and a real photograph from Hubble, Webb or a ground telescope takes over from the dots.

iv

Wind down

A few slow breaths with the wisp, in for four and out for six. The screen dims, and you watch what you set down sink behind the horizon.

Built to let you go

It wants you to leave.

Most apps are built to keep you. This one is built to let you go, and says so, so there's no trick in it.

  • The sky is finite. Only so many things to find each night; then the sky is done until it has turned.
  • It slows as you play. The twinkle, the drift and the pace ease down towards the speed of slow breathing. Nothing announces it.
  • It offers the way out. About a quarter of an hour in, once, a line says how to wind down. It never says how long you've been here.
  • It dims before the end, a little at a time, because bright light keeps a mind awake.
  • Nothing to come back for. No streaks, scores, badges or notifications. Missing a night costs nothing.
  • No network access at all. What you write stays on your computer, by construction.

“If it's clear: Saturn is up in the south-east, a steady light that doesn't twinkle. Give your eyes twenty minutes.”The last line. Then the window closes itself.

A logbook of plain files. A page for each night with a small chart of that evening's sky. Any worry can have a plan charted for it, only if you ask: a wish, the best of it, what gets in the way, and an if-then plan.

The agent monitor

Why it works

Built on what we know about people.

None of this is new science. It's well-studied psychology, applied by software that has nothing to gain from your time.

Where the evidence is modest, we say so: nobody has shown that stargazing itself improves sleep. The claim is only that a little dark and a big sky beat another half hour of screen.

For the technically curious

Under the hood.

Three native Linux apps written in Rust, each its own open-source project, each standing on its own. They share an aim and a look, and no code dependencies in either direction. Every change is built and tested in CI.

Slipstream

Rust · Smithay · Wayland · GTK 4
What it is
A whole desktop: its own Wayland compositor, bar, notifications, lock screen, app explorer and Settings. X11 apps run through XWayland; Flatpaks and games work.
Install
One portable download, checked against its published checksum and signature. It says what it will do before writing anything, and --uninstall removes exactly what it wrote.
Tested on
Every release is installed and started on Debian, Ubuntu, Fedora, Arch and openSUSE.
Extending
Plugins are any program that prints a line of JSON, each in a sandbox that reaches only what it lists. Settings are plain text.

Glimmerwood

Rust · GTK 4 · WebKitGTK 6 · Flatpak
Engine
WebKitGTK, from the same engine family as Safari, in a lean shell with a single toolbar.
Privacy
Trackers blocked before they load; every site asked for a secure connection first. The wisp's records hold a list name, never an address, title or anything typed.
A test for kindness
A test fails the build if the browser's own interface ever grows a pop-up dialog or anything red.
Lists you can read
What counts as restoring or wearing is plain text you can edit, and anyone can propose a change.

Westering

Rust · GTK 4 · Flatpak with no network permission
The sky
Every star to magnitude 6.5 from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue. Tests check the planets against NASA JPL's Horizons to within 0.2°, and the Moon to within 0.1°.
No location service
Where you are comes from your time zone; Settings takes a latitude and longitude if you want it closer.
A test for calm
A test checks that the pace only ever eases down and the light never jumps.
Sources
Over 300 photographs from NASA, ESA/Hubble, ESA/Webb and others, each credited on screen; every music track is CC0.

Not yet: ARM machines, Ubuntu 24.04 and the distributions built on it, and Slipstream with NVIDIA's driver, which hasn't been tried. Glimmerwood is Linux only.

Free software: all three are GPL-3.0-or-later, for x86_64 Linux, on GitHub under peterwalker78. Issues and pull requests are welcome.

Questions

Plain answers.

What is Human Again?
The shared aim of three free Linux apps: Slipstream, a keyboard-first desktop; Glimmerwood, a quiet web browser; and Westering, a night-sky game for winding down. Each is built to give people their attention back rather than to hold on to it.
Are they free?
Yes. All three are free and open source under the GNU GPL, version 3 or later, with no accounts, adverts or paid tiers.
Was the code written by AI?
Yes, all of it, by Claude. The vision, the design decisions and the testing come from Peter Walker, who uses all three apps every day. None of the apps has AI features, and none ever will.
Do they collect any data?
No. There is no telemetry and no account. Glimmerwood's only network traffic is the pages you visit, and Westering has no network permission at all.
Which computers do they run on?
x86_64 Linux. Slipstream needs Debian 13's libraries or newer and is tested on Debian, Ubuntu, Fedora, Arch and openSUSE. Glimmerwood and Westering are Flatpaks for any Linux desktop. There are no Windows or macOS versions.
Can I use one without the others?
Yes. Each is its own project. Slipstream's installer offers the other two, but Glimmerwood and Westering run on any Linux desktop.
Can other apps join?
Yes, if they're free and open source and keep the same promises, checked in the open. One broken promise and they're off the list. Read the code for allies.
